What "today's dollars" means: every spending target on this page (Lean FIRE's $109,500, for example) is stated in today's buying power. The model does not assume you withdraw exactly that many nominal dollars in the future. Instead it inflates the target forward each year (at the inflation rate you set) to find the actual future dollar amount you would need, then compares your projected nominal assets against that inflated number. So $109,500 of Lean FIRE spending means: whatever it costs, years from now, to buy what $109,500 buys today.

Methodology

Exactly how every number on this site is calculated, and where the simplifications are.

Net worth projection engine

Every projection now runs month by month, not year by year. Each month, each person's investable assets compound at your assumed investment return (converted to a monthly-equivalent rate) and that month's contribution, including any employer match and any secondary/side income folded into the income-driven calculation, is added. Contributions stop the month that person reaches their target retirement age, not just the year. Real estate uses standard monthly loan amortization on the actual rate and payment you enter, and the home/rental value grows at your assumed appreciation rate, also converted to a monthly-equivalent rate. Home equity on the primary residence is split 50/50 between you; the rental condo's equity goes entirely to Wes, matching sole ownership. Every projection, including the FIRE cards, Monte Carlo, and the full table, runs out to your "plan to age" setting: specifically, however many months it takes for the later of the two of you to reach that age. The Projection tab's table still displays one row per year for readability, sampled from this monthly engine, but achievement dates everywhere else (FIRE cards, Monte Carlo) are precise to the month.

The three contribution scenarios

Stop investing: contributions are set to zero starting today; assets grow from market returns alone.
Keep current pace: each of you keeps contributing your current monthly amount, flat, every year, until you retire.
Increase over time: your monthly contribution grows, using one of two modes you choose next to the pill:
Flat rate grows your current contribution by a fixed percentage every year (default 3%, loosely tracking how the IRS 401(k) limit tends to rise).
Income-driven instead computes it directly: each year, your household income grows at the income growth rate while your living expenses (from the Budget tab's itemized list, plus mortgage) grow at inflation. Whenever income outgrows expenses, that gap is new disposable money, split between you in proportion to today's income, and added on top of your baseline contribution. It never goes negative if expenses happen to outgrow income; it just adds nothing that year. This extra money is assumed to fill any remaining room under your 401(k) and HSA annual caps first, then spill into brokerage once those are maxed, since brokerage has no contribution limit.
All contribution schedules use the same investment return assumption; they differ only in how much gets contributed.

FIRE numbers, inflation, and "today's dollars"

Every spending target you enter (Lean, Traditional, Chubby, Fat, Barista's offset) is in today's purchasing power. For a given future year, that target is inflated forward: future spending = today's spending × (1 + inflation)^years. Any pension or Social Security income is inflated the same way and subtracted first. What's left is divided by the tax-adjusted safe withdrawal rate (see Tax treatment below) to get that year's required nest egg. Your projected nominal assets are compared against that inflated, tax-adjusted number, not against the plain today's-dollars figure.

Coast FIRE

Calculated differently from the other four. First, the Traditional FIRE number is projected forward to the year Wes turns 65 (inflated spending, minus other income, divided by the tax-adjusted SWR). That future number is then discounted back to today at your investment return rate: coast number = future Traditional number ÷ (1 + return)^years to Wes's retirement. That's the lump sum that, left alone with zero further contributions, would grow to fully fund Traditional FIRE by age 65 on market returns alone. Progress and "achieved" dates for Coast compare your assets against this shrinking-in-time-remaining bar, recalculated for every year in the projection.

Tax treatment (simplified)

This is a gross-up, not real bucket-by-bucket account tracking. The pre-tax share is computed automatically from your account balances on the Inputs tab (traditional 401(k)/IRA balances ÷ total investable assets). "Effective tax rate" and "state tax rate" are added together into one blended rate you'd expect to pay on withdrawals from the pre-tax share. Every required nest egg is then inflated to cover that expected tax drag: tax-adjusted SWR = SWR × (1 − pre-tax share × (federal rate + state rate)), and that adjusted rate is used everywhere in place of the raw safe withdrawal rate. The same combined federal-plus-state rate is also applied to income in the Budget tab's tax estimate. Filing status is currently stored and displayed only. It doesn't change any calculation yet, since this tool doesn't model tax brackets. It does not model Roth conversion ladders or account-specific withdrawal ordering either.

Health care bridge

If either of you retires before 65, your entered monthly premium (inflated each year) is added on top of that year's spending target, only for the years between your retirement age and 65. At 65, it's assumed Medicare takes over and the premium drops out.

Monte Carlo simulation

Runs 300 independent simulated futures, month by month. Each month gets its own random investment return drawn from a normal distribution generated with a standard Box-Muller transform, centered on your assumed return converted to a monthly-equivalent mean, with the monthly standard deviation set by scaling your volatility input by the square root of 12 (the standard way to convert annual volatility to monthly). Both of your combined investable assets share the same randomly drawn return in a given month in a given simulation, since a real market month doesn't move your accounts independently. Contributions follow whichever scenario pill is selected on the Dashboard, including the flat-rate or income-driven mode if "Increase over time" is active. For each FIRE type, "chance of success" is the share of the 300 simulations where combined assets crossed that type's (inflated, tax-adjusted) required number within the plan horizon. "Typical month" is the median month and year among only the simulations that succeeded. The shaded band on the chart spans the 10th to 90th percentile of simulated net worth at each point; the dark line is the median (50th percentile) path.

Debt payoff (avalanche, snowball & minimums only)

Simulated month by month. Each month, every debt accrues interest on its remaining balance at its stated annual rate divided by 12, then a payment is applied. Two separate payment figures are tracked per debt: the required minimum and what you're actually paying, which can be higher if you're already voluntarily overpaying something. Under avalanche or snowball, each debt's actual payment is applied first, then any extra monthly payment you've set, plus the full payment amount of any debt that's already been paid off, is pooled together and thrown at whichever debt is "first": avalanche orders by highest interest rate first (minimizes total interest paid); snowball orders by smallest remaining balance first (clears individual debts faster). That's the classic "debt snowball" rolling effect: freed-up payments permanently join the extra-payment pool once a debt is gone. Under minimums only, every debt gets exactly its minimum payment and nothing else. The extra-payment input and the rolling-freed-payments mechanic are both switched off entirely, showing the slowest, most conservative payoff baseline with no acceleration at all.

Budget and cash flow

Built around a real itemized expense list (add, edit, or remove any line) rather than a rough estimate. "Essential living" sums every expense tagged essential, plus the primary mortgage's P&I and escrow pulled in automatically from Real Estate. "Discretionary" sums everything tagged discretionary. Income is your two salaries (annual ÷ 12) plus monthly rental income. Taxes are estimated as a flat combined federal-plus-state percentage of gross income (editable), not a real bracket calculation. Debt payments sum the monthly payments on the Debt tab. Investing is your combined current monthly contributions, including employer match. Rental costs are the rental property's P&I, escrow, and other monthly expenses. Whatever's left (or missing) after all of that is shown as surplus or shortfall. This same itemized total is what "income-driven" contribution growth (see above) uses as the spending baseline that grows with inflation.

Savings goals

Each goal is independent of the FIRE machinery and the emergency fund. Months remaining is calculated from today's date to your target date. Monthly savings needed is simply (target amount − current amount) ÷ months remaining, floored at the full remaining amount if the target date has already passed. No investment growth is assumed on money set aside for a goal; it's treated as cash you're accumulating, not investing.

Net worth history

Purely a logging tool; nothing here feeds back into any projection. Clicking "Add snapshot" pre-fills today's date and today's actual figures pulled live from your accounts and real estate equity elsewhere in the tool, so you're not re-typing numbers that already exist. Every field is editable afterward if you want to log a different value or backfill an earlier date. The chart plots your logged points directly against the "keep current pace" projected line for the same dates, so drift between plan and reality is visible at a glance.

Estate basics

A checklist only. Nothing on this tab is calculated, projected, or used anywhere else in the tool.
What this tool is not: it isn't tax software, doesn't model account-specific withdrawal ordering or Roth conversion strategy, doesn't do real bracket-based tax calculations, and its Monte Carlo engine uses one simplified random-return draw per month rather than modeling actual asset-class behavior or sequence-of-returns nuance beyond that. Treat every number here as a planning estimate to sanity-check decisions against, not as financial or tax advice.
